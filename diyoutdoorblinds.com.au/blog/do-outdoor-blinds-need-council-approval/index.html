Do outdoor blinds need council approval in Australia?

For most homes, adding blinds to an existing verandah or pergola is not a planning matter. The exceptions are specific, and worth checking before you spend money.

Approvals · 6 min read · Updated November 2026

Short answer: Usually not. Outdoor blinds fitted to an existing, approved verandah or pergola are generally treated as minor work in most Australian councils. Approval may be needed for heritage-listed properties, in bushfire-prone areas, in strata buildings, or where blinds fully enclose a space. Rules vary by state and council, so check locally.

This is general information, not legal or planning advice. Planning rules are set by each state and applied by local councils, and they change. The aim here is to help you work out which questions to ask, and who to ask.

When are outdoor blinds usually exempt?

Across Australia, outdoor blinds attached to a structure that already exists and already has any approval it needed are commonly treated as minor or exempt work. The blind is seen as a fitting on the structure rather than a new building. That typically covers:

  • Blinds on an existing verandah, pergola or patio cover
  • Blinds that roll up and can be opened, rather than permanently enclosing the space
  • Blinds on a single dwelling not affected by heritage, bushfire or strata controls

The key word is existing. If the pergola or patio itself is new, it may need its own approval, and the blinds would be considered alongside it.

Why do the rules vary so much?

Each state and territory sets its own planning framework, and each council then applies its own local controls on top. A blind that needs no paperwork in one suburb can need a quick check in the next because a heritage overlay, a bushfire map or a local character control applies. That is why the same question gets different answers on forums — people are describing different councils.

The practical takeaway is not to rely on what a neighbour in another council area was told. The answer that matters is the one for your property, which is why the online property report or a short call to your own council is worth doing.

Heritage overlays

If your home is heritage-listed, or sits in a heritage conservation area, the council may control changes that are visible from the street — sometimes including colours and materials. Blinds at the back of the house are less likely to be an issue than blinds on a front verandah. Your council's planning certificate or online property report will usually show whether a heritage control applies.

Bushfire-prone areas

In areas mapped as bushfire-prone, homes are assigned a Bushfire Attack Level (BAL), and building work may need to meet construction requirements for that level. Whether and how this applies to outdoor blinds depends on your state, your BAL rating and how the blind is attached. If your property has a BAL rating, ask your council before ordering, and ask your supplier what fabrics are available. Do not assume a standard mesh or PVC blind is suitable.

Strata and body corporate

Strata approval is separate from council approval and is the one most often needed. In apartments and many townhouse complexes, anything that changes the outside of the building generally requires approval under the scheme's by-laws, even when council does not care. Get it in writing before ordering.

Does enclosing a verandah make it a room?

This is the grey area. Roll-up blinds that open and close are generally treated as blinds. But if a verandah is permanently enclosed — with fixed walls, doors or glazing — it may be treated as habitable space, which brings different building requirements. Keep the blinds operable, and if your plan starts to involve fixed panels or doors, talk to a building certifier.

What about blinds on a rental property?

If you rent, council approval is rarely the hurdle — your landlord's consent is. Most residential tenancy agreements require written permission for fixtures that involve drilling. Put the request in writing, describe the blind and how it will be fixed, and agree whether it stays when you leave. If you own a rental, the same planning considerations apply as for your own home.

Is there a difference between awnings and blinds?

Sometimes, for planning purposes. A blind that drops vertically under an existing roof is usually treated as a minor fitting. A folding-arm or fixed awning that projects out from a wall can be treated more like a structure, particularly if it projects over a boundary, a footpath or a public space, or covers a large area. If what you are planning projects outwards rather than dropping down, check with the council even if blinds alone would not need it.

How to check in ten minutes

  1. Look up your property on your council's website — most have an online planning map or property report showing heritage and bushfire overlays.
  2. If you are in a strata scheme, email the strata manager.
  3. If anything is unclear, call the council's duty planner with a short description: "roll-up outdoor blinds on an existing pergola at the rear of a single house." That one sentence usually gets a quick answer.
  4. Keep any written confirmation with your order paperwork.

Tip: describe exactly what you are doing. "Enclosing the verandah" and "adding roll-up blinds to the verandah" can get very different answers from a planner.

Common questions

Do I need a permit for outdoor blinds in Australia?

Usually not, if the blinds are fitted to an existing approved verandah or pergola on a single house. Rules vary by state and council, so check if your property has heritage, bushfire or strata controls.

Do outdoor blinds need council approval in a heritage area?

They might, especially if they are visible from the street. Check with your council's planning or heritage team before ordering.

Can I install outdoor blinds in a bushfire zone?

Often yes, but your property's BAL rating may affect which materials are suitable. Ask your council and your supplier before ordering.

Does enclosing a patio with blinds need approval?

Roll-up blinds that can be opened are generally treated as blinds. Permanently enclosing a patio with fixed walls or doors may be treated as creating a room, which can need approval.

Who do I ask about outdoor blind approval?

Your local council for planning, heritage and bushfire questions, and your strata manager or owners corporation if you live in a strata building.
